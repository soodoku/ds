Underlying Reasons

  • Training data can reflect unequal representation, selection, or measurement
    • CEO Barbie
      • captures societal biases
    • Hiring
  • An unweighted aggregate loss gives more influence to groups with more observations
    • Good average performance can conceal poor performance for a smaller group
  • Observed base rates can differ across groups
    • Such differences do not, by themselves, establish intrinsic causes; consider measurement, selection, and social conditions

What’s Fair?

  • Fairness:
    • Who gets a loan and who doesn’t
    • Even perfect prediction of a recorded label does not settle whether that label, decision, or allocation is fair
    • Misclassifications

Group fairness

  • Specify the rate and its denominator:
    • Accuracy conditions on group membership.
    • False-positive rate: \(P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=0,G=g)\).
    • True-positive rate: \(P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=1,G=g)\).
  • Equal overall error rates do not imply equal false-positive or false-negative rates.
  • Equalized odds requires equal FPR and TPR across groups. Equal opportunity requires equal TPR.
  • A group-independent coin flip can satisfy some parity criteria while making poor decisions. No one metric settles fairness.
  • Measurement matters: which groups, which labels, and whose harms?

Solutions

  • Anti-classification

    • Omit sensitive attributes, but recognize that other features can act as proxies; omission alone does not ensure fairness

  • Calibration

  • Classification Parity

    • via constrained optimization
      • constraint: group error rates are the same
      • When we are doing param. search, our search space is restricted
      • satisficing vs. optimizing metric
        • constraint = satisficing metric
    • via multi-objective optimization
      • pareto frontier
        • solutions where no specified objective can improve without worsening another specified objective
    • Weight examples from underrepresented groups when appropriate; outcome classes and demographic groups are different
    • Train separate models for different groups

Trade-offs

  • Equalizing one error metric may worsen another; compare the actual false positives, false negatives, and consequences for each group.
  • Example: moving from 10 and 5 errors per 100 people to 20 and 20 equalizes overall error while harming both groups.
  • A parity constraint is a mathematical requirement, not a complete definition of fair treatment.
  • With different base rates, calibration and equalized error rates generally conflict except in special cases such as perfect prediction.

Lender’s perspective

  • Classification error alone does not determine interest rates or loan access.
  • Consequences depend on who is wrongly denied, who receives an unaffordable loan, default losses, decision thresholds, and market conditions.
  • Evaluate the chosen policy’s outcomes for applicants as well as lenders.
  • Predictions describe risks under the observed process; changing lending policy can change who receives a loan and which outcomes are observed.

Uncertain

  • Discriminating against people where we are most uncertain
    • wider confidence bands for members in group x vs. group y on average
    • wide confidence bands mean
      • the estimated probability is imprecise; a wide interval does not by itself imply bias
        • uncertainty about the estimated probability is distinct from the randomness of an individual outcome
  • Solution
    • if confidence_bands are big:
      • collect more data or compensate in another way
